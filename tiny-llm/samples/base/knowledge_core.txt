Scientific reasoning and practical knowledge

The scientific method is a disciplined way to reduce error. A useful loop is:
observe, propose a hypothesis, make a testable prediction, run a controlled
test, and update the hypothesis based on evidence. Good scientific writing
states assumptions, methods, limitations, and confidence level.

Probability is not certainty. A forecast with 70 percent confidence means that
if we repeated similar conditions many times, the event would occur around 70
times out of 100. Good decisions combine probability, impact, and reversibility.

Statistics answers "how likely is this pattern by chance?" A low p-value does
not prove a claim. Effect size and data quality matter as much as significance.
Biased sampling, leakage, and poor measurement can produce precise but wrong
numbers.

In machine learning, train and validation data must be separated. Leakage often
happens when labels or future information enter input features. A model may look
excellent in offline metrics and fail in production because real data drifted.

Data quality basics:
1. Define schema and units.
2. Enforce required fields.
3. Remove exact and near-duplicate records when needed.
4. Track data source, version, and timestamp.
5. Build simple sanity checks before large training runs.

Computer systems are trade-offs. CPU focuses on low-latency general tasks. GPU
focuses on high-throughput parallel math. Memory bandwidth and storage speed can
be bottlenecks even when compute is available.

For reliable software systems:
1. Prefer clear interfaces over implicit coupling.
2. Keep side effects explicit.
3. Log structured events, not only plain text.
4. Add health checks and timeout boundaries.
5. Design for restart and partial failure.

A practical debugging method:
1. Reproduce the issue in the smallest possible setup.
2. Add checkpoints to confirm assumptions.
3. Change one variable at a time.
4. Compare expected vs observed behavior with concrete evidence.
5. Write a short postmortem and prevent recurrence.

Security basics apply everywhere:
- Validate and normalize inputs.
- Use least privilege.
- Avoid hardcoded secrets.
- Rotate credentials and audit access.
- Treat user data as sensitive by default.

Networking essentials:
- Latency is time per request.
- Throughput is total work per unit time.
- Retries improve resilience but can amplify load.
- Backoff and jitter reduce synchronized spikes.
- Caching helps when data is read frequently and updated rarely.

Energy and climate knowledge:
Power is instantaneous rate of energy use. Energy is accumulated power over
time. Efficiency gains reduce cost, but rebound effects can increase total
consumption if demand rises faster than efficiency.

In communication, clarity beats complexity. A strong technical explanation
includes goal, constraints, design choice, trade-off, and next action. Avoid
claims without a measurable criterion.

A high-quality engineering culture is evidence-driven. Teams should prefer
small reproducible experiments, clear ownership, and post-release monitoring.
Good process makes quality repeatable rather than accidental.
